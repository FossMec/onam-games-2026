# FOSS Onam Games: Visual Design Language

## Purpose

This document is the visual source of truth for the FOSS Onam Games design system. It defines the art direction, the six non-negotiable rules, the palette, typography, shape vocabulary, motifs, motion, and even the cursor. Every screen, illustration, decorative asset, thumbnail, background, and piece of character art follows this.

It is not a generic Kerala festival brief and it is not a generic retro-gaming brief. The project has a deliberately specific visual identity:

> **Washed 1990s comic-book print meets Kerala Onam iconography meets Memphis geometry meets Linux-club mischief.**

The work should feel made by a clever student collective with strong taste, limited resources, and a photocopied comic annual on the desk. It should be playful and loud without becoming glossy, corporate, neon, or childish in a polished-cartoon way.

The most important quality is the double reading. To someone unfamiliar with Kerala, the imagery can initially read as pop art or Memphis design. To a Malayali, the same shapes should quietly resolve into pookalam, vallam, muthukuda, banana leaf, coconut palm, Maveli, and Onam celebration.

## One-Sentence Art Direction

Create flat, hand-inked, slightly irregular comic-book artwork on warm cream newsprint, using washed pastel festival colours, radial pookalam structures, chunky playful forms, visible halftone dots, and a mischievous Kerala/FOSS personality.

## Inspiration: Memphis × Comic Book Print

The identity is a marriage of two printed-design movements that already agree on the fundamentals - flat colour, hard outlines, and pattern.

### 1980s Memphis

The Memphis Group (Ettore Sottsass and friends) built loud, playful interiors from abstract geometric shapes: squiggles, zigzags, dots, rounded arcs, and chunky forms in soothing pastel-faded colour fields. The shapes are naive on purpose. They feel hand-drawn even when they are tiled.

### Comic-book print

Thick inked contours, Ben-Day halftone dots, starbursts, speech bubbles, tilted stickers, and a photocopied-annual imperfection. Everything reads as something physically printed and handled, never rendered on a screen.

### Why they share a language

Both use flat colour, confident outlines, and pattern to carry emotion. The Onam layer then quietly replaces every abstract Memphis shape with a Kerala motif - so the same geometry reads as pop-art to outsiders and as festival to Malayalis. That overlap is the whole trick: the decoration is never empty, but it never needs explaining.

## The Six Non-Negotiable Rules

### 1. No conventional shadows

Do not use soft drop shadows, realistic cast shadows, glossy bevels, glassmorphism, or three-dimensional lighting as the default depth treatment.

Depth comes from:

- confident dark ink outlines (thicker line = closer)
- overlapping flat colour blocks
- a second offset flat shape, like printed poster registration
- Ben-Day halftone dots
- occasional darker colour regions, still flat and graphic

If an asset needs to feel raised, use a thicker outline, a coloured offset layer, or dots. Never solve it with a blurred grey shadow.

### 2. Halftone dots are a pookalam

Ben-Day dots are not incidental texture. They are the signature pop-art shading language, redesigned as radial pookalam symmetry. Ours are laid out in radial flower-carpet rings instead of a sterile square computer grid, so the pop-art motif and the Onam motif are the same object.

Use dots as:

- concentric radial rings
- patterned shading inside a flat colour area
- sparse comic-print texture
- a subtle page-wide background field
- a press-print imperfection that makes the art feel physical

### 3. Memphis decoration secretly means Onam

Decorative geometry should carry Kerala meaning without becoming literal clip art.

- A long zigzag is a **vallam**, or snake boat.
- Concentric circles are a **pookalam**.
- A half-circle with a stem is a **muthukuda**, the ceremonial umbrella.
- An organic blob is an **ila**, a banana leaf.
- A loose curved line is a **thengu**, a coconut palm.
- A diamond kite can suggest festival play and paper-cut Memphis geometry.
- Small dot clusters suggest flowers, confetti, and print texture at once.

Use these translations rather than decorating every surface with literal flowers, elephants, temple architecture, or stock festival symbols.

### 4. Everything is inked

Every important object should look outlined by one confident warm near-black marker or screen-print block. Use the same ink family for:

- borders
- character contours
- buttons and badges
- puzzle pieces
- boats and platforms
- speech bubbles
- stars and bursts
- small decorative icons

Do not mix delicate grey hairlines with heavy comic outlines. Do not use pure blue-black. The ink is warm, nearly black (`#22202b`), and slightly softer than absolute black.

### 5. Soothing pastel-faded, never neon

The palette should resemble a well-printed comic annual or risograph poster that has been handled for years. Colours are saturated enough to be joyful, but all sit in a similar, soothing lightness range - pastel, faded, and slightly sun-washed rather than electric.

Avoid electric cyan, laser magenta, acid green, pure red, digital royal blue, chrome, and fluorescent yellow. The palette should clash harmoniously, not scream like an esports landing page.

### 6. Tilt is punctuation

Small rotations make the composition feel collaged, physical, and human. Use one or two degrees of tilt on stickers, labels, bursts, and marginal notes.

Never rotate anything that must be read quickly or compared precisely:

- puzzle boards
- game grids
- timers
- leaderboard rows
- measurement labels
- input controls

## Emotional Target

The image should make someone think:

- "This looks fun."
- "Someone here has a sense of humour."
- "I recognise the Onam reference, but it is not being explained to me."
- "This feels handmade, not cheaply generated."
- "I want to tap this."

It should not make someone think:

- "This is a corporate Indian festival campaign."
- "This is a generic mobile game ad."
- "This is a glossy 3D mascot brand."
- "This is a stock Kerala tourism poster."
- "This is random AI cartoon art with no system."

## Material and Surface

### Base surface

Use warm cream newsprint as the default ground. It should be close to an old ivory paper, not white and not beige cardboard. The page is part of the identity and should remain visible around the artwork.

### Print character

Images may include:

- very subtle paper grain
- restrained ink spread
- tiny registration misalignment
- imperfect but intentional outline wobble
- faint halftone density changes
- flat colour patches with a screen-print feel

The imperfections must be controlled. The artwork should feel printed, not dirty, distressed, noisy, or vintage-themed for its own sake.

### Line quality

Use a bold, rounded, hand-inked contour. Lines can vary slightly in weight, especially around expressive characters and starbursts, but should never become sketchy concept art. The silhouette must remain legible at small sizes.

## Colour System

### Core neutrals

- **Newsprint cream:** `#FBF3E4`. The main page and the identity colour.
- **Raised paper:** `#FFFCF5`. Clean interior panels and light surfaces.
- **Sunken paper:** `#F0E4CC`. Muted areas, trays, footer surfaces, and quieter backgrounds.
- **Warm ink:** `#22202B`. All primary outlines, lettering, and structural marks.
- **Soft ink:** `#6B6478`. Secondary copy and subdued annotations only.

### Soothing pastel accents

Six accents tuned to near-identical lightness, so they clash harmoniously and feel sun-washed rather than neon:

- **Tomato red:** `#F2695C`. Failure, energy, expressive marks, and the main Maveli face.
- **Festival yellow:** `#F5C443`. Celebration, highlights, stars, crown, primary emphasis.
- **Pookalam teal:** `#5FBFA8`. Brand colour, safe states, open source, mint platforms.
- **Washed blue:** `#7B9BE0`. Information, motion, calm structure, moving platforms.
- **Flower pink:** `#E48BB4`. Pookalam petals, playful secondary emphasis, bounce platforms.
- **Muted purple:** `#9C82D4`. Mystery, depth in the flat palette, supporting pookalam rings.

### Colour behaviour

Use two to four accent colours in one asset, not all six by default. Let cream and ink occupy enough space to keep the image breathable. The colour system should feel like a family of screen-printed inks, not a rainbow gradient.

Prefer:

- large flat fields
- one dominant accent
- one contrasting accent
- ink as the visual anchor
- cream as negative space

Avoid:

- gradients as the primary form language
- transparent glossy overlays
- photorealistic colour variation
- dark mode palettes
- black backgrounds with glowing neon accents

## Typography Direction

The type system has seven distinct voices, each with exactly one job. That discipline is what keeps a multi-font system from reading like a ransom note.

- **Bungee:** the logo and wordmark. Heavy, condensed, poster-like, layered with a flat yellow offset behind warm ink. Reserved exclusively for the FOSS ONAM wordmark.
- **Baloo Chettan 2:** headings, labels, buttons, and display copy. Rounded, heavy, friendly, and highly legible.
- **Nunito:** body text. Soft, readable, generous, and practical on a phone.
- **Space Mono:** numbers, timers, scores, technical measurements, and leaderboard data.
- **Bangers:** comic shouts and onomatopoeia. Use for explosive result moments, not normal headings.
- **Caveat:** handwritten marginal commentary and sarcastic asides. It should feel like a note scribbled beside the panel.
- **Kalam:** proclamations and royal letters, like the letter from Maveli. A warmer, more formal hand than Caveat.

For generated image compositions, visualise these roles even when no type is present: poster wordmark, rounded display label, readable body block, technical mono number, explosive comic shout, and hand-written annotation.

## Shape Vocabulary

### Panels and containers

Panels are boxy but softly rounded, like comic frames and printed cards. They have a thick ink boundary and a flat interior. A panel may carry a narrow coloured strip along its top edge, which separates it from the paper without a shadow.

### Stickers

Stickers are compact, uppercase, outlined labels with a slight rotation. They look physically slapped onto a page. Keep them simple and high contrast.

### Badges

Badges are smaller, pill-shaped, outlined markers. They are more functional than stickers and should feel tidy, compact, and repeatable.

### Starbursts

Starbursts are irregular rather than mathematically perfect. Use uneven spike lengths and slight angle variation so they feel drawn, not copied from a clip-art library. A secondary larger translucent ink burst may sit behind the main coloured burst, but this is still a flat print layer, not a shadow.

### Speech bubbles

Speech bubbles have a continuous ink outline and a simple integrated tail. They should feel like a comic panel object, not a chat-app interface.

### Organic shapes

Use banana-leaf blobs, loose palm squiggles, rounded petals, and soft asymmetrical silhouettes to interrupt the geometry. Organic does not mean painterly. Keep the fills flat and the contours inked.

## Onam Motif Library

### Pookalam

The pookalam is the central visual metaphor. It should be radial, layered, symmetric, and intricate enough to reward looking closely.

Recommended construction:

- broad concentric rings
- alternating bands of teal, purple, cream, red, yellow, blue, and pink
- repeated petals or arrow-like forms around a centre
- a small central flower or dot cluster
- thick ink between major rings
- occasional halftone ring or dot field
- near-symmetry with only slight printed irregularity

For main artwork, favour a strong central axis and a readable silhouette at thumbnail size. For texture, use cropped sections of rings and petals rather than always showing a complete mandala.

### Vallam

The chundan vallam should be communicated with a long, low, slightly exaggerated silhouette. It can be represented as a zigzag, an upturned prow, a row of repeating marks, or a red hero boat among blocked lanes.

Do not render a realistic wooden boat. Use the idea of a snake boat translated into a chunky comic puzzle token.

### Muthukuda

Use a half-circle canopy with a simple vertical stem. It can become a parasol, a dome, a punctuation mark, or a repeated festival shape. Keep it graphic and flat.

### Banana leaf

Translate the banana leaf into a large friendly blob or rounded leaf shape. It can be a background silhouette, a plate-like stage, or a framing device. It should not look botanical or photorealistic.

### Coconut palm

Use a loose, winding, slightly silly squiggle rather than a detailed palm illustration. A few curved strokes are enough to establish the reference.

### Maveli

Maveli is a comic game character, not a mythological painting. He must remain recognisable at small sizes:

- round pot belly
- red head and face area
- curled or simple moustache
- yellow crown
- bold warm ink contour
- compact, readable silhouette

Keep his expression cheerful, determined, or mildly embarrassed. His dignity should be slightly under pressure, matching the project's humour. Do not depict him as a hyper-realistic king, a warrior, a deity, or a generic superhero.

## The Muthukuda Cursor

Even the pointer is part of the identity. The site swaps the system arrow for a muthukuda - the ceremonial umbrella - tilted so it reads as a pointer rather than a sticker parked on the page.

Three tilts of the same umbrella carry the three interaction states, so the shape stays consistent while still telling people what they can do:

- **-18deg** default, resting
- **-38deg** over anything clickable, leaning in
- **+24deg** grabbing, leaning back

The hotspot sits on the canopy's top-left, which is where the eye reads the point to be. Text inputs keep the system I-beam: a caret is a precision instrument and an umbrella cannot show which character you are between. Disabled controls keep the system not-allowed cursor.

## Composition Principles

### Build from a hero silhouette

Every asset needs one primary readable shape: Maveli, a pookalam, a vallam, a game token, a burst, or a festival object. Secondary motifs should support it rather than compete with it.

### Use visible negative space

Cream space is an active design element. Leave breathing room around the hero object and around any expected interface text. Avoid filling every corner with confetti.

### Use asymmetry inside symmetry

Pookalam structures can be mathematically radial, but surrounding decoration should be uneven. A balanced centre with a slightly off-kilter sticker or scattered motif feels more human than a perfect tiled pattern.

### Make the crop useful

For hero assets, generate versions that can survive:

- wide desktop banners
- square cards
- tall mobile panels
- small thumbnails

Keep critical character faces and pookalam centres away from edges. Prefer broad zones of low detail where responsive cropping can happen.

### Keep gameplay readable

Artwork used behind or around a game board must remain quiet. No decorative shape should look like an interactive tile, answer, target, or timer. Gameplay surfaces use flat paper, ink, and restrained colour blocks; decorative richness belongs outside the board.

## Motion Implied by Still Images

Even static assets should suggest gentle, ambient motion:

- confetti pieces drifting at different angles
- a starburst caught at the instant of impact
- a boat leaning into its escape
- Maveli mid-jump with crown and moustache intact
- a swipe card slightly tilted
- a traced word path curving through a grid

Do not imply chaotic motion blur, speed-line overload, or violent camera movement. The interface uses subtle movement and gameplay surfaces stay stable.

## Decorative Confetti Rules

Confetti should feel like Memphis scatter that happens to encode Onam. Use a small number of well-spaced pieces rather than a dense party explosion.

Good pieces include:

- a low zigzag vallam
- concentric pookalam rings
- a muthukuda half-circle
- a banana-leaf blob
- a palm squiggle
- an outlined diamond kite
- compact coloured dot clusters

Vary size, rotation, and position. Avoid repeating one exact icon in a regular grid. Decorative pieces should sit behind content, never cover buttons, faces, puzzle instructions, or score data.

## What Not to Generate

Reject or revise any output containing:

- soft drop shadows or floating card shadows
- glossy 3D rendering, realistic materials, or cinematic lighting
- neon cyberpunk colours
- a generic modern SaaS illustration style
- gradient-heavy vector art
- photorealistic Kerala scenery
- stock festival imagery
- ornate temple poster styling as the main language
- generic cartoon mascots with no Onam/FOSS cue
- perfect sterile geometry with no print character
- dense visual noise that competes with gameplay
- random illegible text or fake interface labels
- generic "POW", "BAM", or American superhero comic clichés
- overuse of elephants, kathakali faces, lamps, or other literal cultural shorthand
- a dark theme or black canvas as the dominant surface
- realistic flowers in place of graphic pookalam geometry
- excessive distress, grunge, paper tears, or fake aging

## Asset Prompt Template

Use this structure when commissioning or generating an asset:

**Subject:** the single hero object or scene.

**Onam translation:** which Kerala motif is being encoded, if any.

**Context:** where the asset appears and what must remain readable around it.

**Composition:** hero silhouette, crop safety, negative space, and whether the asset is radial, horizontal, vertical, or modular.

**Visual treatment:** flat washed pastel comic-print colour, warm ink outline, controlled halftone, slight hand-drawn irregularity, no conventional shadow.

**Palette:** choose one dominant accent, one supporting accent, cream, and warm ink from the approved palette.

**Surface:** clean newsprint with restrained print texture, not grunge.

**Mood:** playful, mischievous, approachable, and locally specific.

**Exclusions:** list anything that would make the asset too realistic, too glossy, too noisy, too neon, or too culturally literal.

## Final Review Checklist

Before approving an asset, ask:

- Does it look like washed comic print on cream paper?
- Is the main silhouette readable at thumbnail size?
- Is the object outlined with the shared warm ink language?
- Is depth communicated without a blurred shadow?
- Are the colours soothing pastel-faded and harmonious rather than neon?
- Is there a clear Onam reference, direct or encoded?
- Does the image still feel like FOSS Onam Games rather than generic pop art?
- Is there enough negative space for UI and responsive cropping?
- Would a Malayali recognise an extra layer of meaning?
- Would the asset remain clear beside a phone-sized game board?
- Is any text exact, intentional, and supplied separately where necessary?
- Does the artwork feel playful and mischievous without becoming noisy or childish?

If the answer to several of these is no, simplify the asset. This design system is strongest when a few bold, specific shapes do more work than a highly detailed illustration.
